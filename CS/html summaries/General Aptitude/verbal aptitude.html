1. Pronouns & Relative Clauses

flowchart LR A[Relative Pronoun] --> B{Object Type?} B -->|Humans| C[Who / Whom] B -->|Infants/Objects/Animals| D[Which] B -->|Selection| E[Which] B -->|Restrictive/All types| F[That]

Who vs. Whom

Objective Case Omission: The relative pronoun in the objective case is generally omitted.

The student whom you wanted to punish...
The student you wanted to punish...

Usage of 'Which'

  • Infants, Objects, Small Animals: "This is the baby which was lost."
  • Selection: "Which of these watches do you want?"
  • Referring to a Situation: "He was drunk, which was not true."

Usage of 'That'

Used for persons or things (singular or plural). Mandatory after "The same", "All", "Nothing", "Only".

This is the girl that failed the exam.
The weather of Hyderabad is better than that of Mumbai.

2. Adjectives & Degrees of Comparison

Standard Progression

$$ \text{Positive (as...as)} \rightarrow \text{Comparative (er...than)} \rightarrow \text{Superlative (the...est)} $$

Rule: If selecting between two of the same kind, use "the better of".

She is the better of the two sisters.

Critical Rules & Exceptions

1. Two qualities in one person

Use more instead of -er.

Rekha is wiser than intelligent.
Rekha is more wise than intelligent.

2. Comparison with Group (Any Other)

When comparing one item to others in the same group, "other" is mandatory.

Gold is more precious than any metal.
Gold is more precious than any other metal.

3. Latin Adjectives (The 'OR' ending)

Followed by TO, not THAN.

Words: Superior, Senior, Junior, Inferior, Prior, Anterior, Prefer.

He is senior than me.
He is senior to me.

4. Non-Gradable Adjectives

Cannot be Comparative or Superlative.

Words: Unique, Universal, Round, Perfect, Dead, Complete, Empty.

Most unique.
Unique.

3. Adverbs

graph TD A[Adverb Position] A --> B[Just/Only] B --> C[Placed immediately BEFORE the word modified] A --> D[Frequency Often/Always] D --> E[Between Auxiliary and Main Verb]

Placement Rule

I have told him often...
I have often told him...
I only solved two problems. (Ambiguous)
I solved only two problems.

Hard vs. Hardly

  • Hard: Diligently/Tough (Adjective or Adverb). "He works hard."
  • Hardly: Scarcely/Barely (Negative Adverb). "He hardly works."

Inversion Rule

Scarcely/Hardly followed by "when".
No sooner followed by "than".

$$ \text{No sooner} + \text{Had/Did} + \text{Sub} + \text{Verb} $$

No sooner had I reached... than the train left.

4. Subject-Verb Agreement (SVA)

The Golden Rules of Singular vs. Plural

graph TD A[Subject Structure] --> B{Joined by AND} B -->|Different entities| C(Plural Verb) B -->|Same Idea/Person| D(Singular Verb) A --> E{Many/Each/Every} E -->|Many a + Sing Noun| F(Singular Verb) E -->|A great many + Plural| G(Plural Verb) A --> H{Or / Nor / But also} H --> I(Verb agrees with NEAREST Subject)
Construction Verb Type Example
Article Rule (The X and Y) Singular "The District Magistrate and Collector is on leave." (One person holding two posts).
Article Rule (The X and The Y) Plural "The Secretary and the President have been given..." (Two people).
Unified Idea Singular "Bread and milk is good." / "Slow and steady wins."
Each / Every Singular "Every boy and girl was present."
Many a... Singular "Many a scientist is attending."
A number of / A lot of Plural (if number) "A lot of people were present."
Majority Depends Alone: Singular ("The majority believes").
With noun: Plural ("The majority of students were").

5. Conditionals (If-Clauses)

The verb in the main clause depends entirely on the tense used in the 'If' clause.

Type If Clause (Condition) Main Clause (Result) Example
Real/Future Present Simple Future (Will + V1) "If he comes, I will give books."
Hypothetical Past Simple Would + V1 "If she came, I would give."
Past Impossible Past Perfect (Had + V3) Would + Have + V3 "If she had come, I would have given."
Pure Imagination Were (always) Would + V1 "If I were a bird, I would fly."

6. Prepositions

Movement & Position

  • In: Static/Rest. "She is in the garden."
  • Into: Motion. "He walked into the garden."
  • On: Rest. "Sat on a stone."
  • Upon: Motion. "Tiger sprang upon the goat."

Time & Groups

  • In (Time): End of a period (Future). "Return in a month."
  • Within: Before end of period. "Return within a month."
  • Between: Two persons/things.
  • Among: More than two.

Gerunds after Prepositions

Certain words + preposition are followed by V-ing.

  • Refrain from hurting
  • Bent upon doing
  • Adverse to playing
  • Succeed in doing
  • Insist on going

7. Critical Reasoning & Practice Logic

Inference Rules

Based on lecture examples (Rabies, Tourism passages):

  1. Strict Scope: Do not assume outside knowledge. If the text says "70% vaccination reduces cases," you cannot infer "Stray dogs are the main source worldwide" unless explicitly stated.
  2. Quantity Words: Pay attention to "Most", "Some", "All", "A few".
  3. Causality: "A leads to B" does not mean "B is the only outcome of A".
Text: "inoculating 70% of pets... can lead to reduction in rabies."
Valid Inference: The number of people currently infected is high (implied by the need for reduction).
